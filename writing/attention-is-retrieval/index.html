Attention is a retrieval problem wearing a different name

If the attention used in transformers is exactly the update rule of a modern associative memory, then its cost is a property of one implementation rather than a property of relevance itself.

Mateo Hernandez · 9 August 2026

The mechanism that made transformers work is usually described as something new. R002 argues it is not. The update rule of a modern Hopfield network, run for a single step, is the same expression as the key and value attention of R001.

That equivalence is worth more than a historical footnote. It means the quadratic cost of attention is not the price of selecting what is relevant. It is the price of one particular way of selecting it, in which every stored item is compared against every query. An associative memory that is addressed by a cue rather than scanned pays a different price entirely.

What the capacity actually is#

The classical result is modest. In a network of a thousand dimensions, the number of patterns recoverable without error is a thousand divided by twice the natural logarithm of a thousand, which is 72.382413650541971 patterns. The modern continuous version does far better, and R002 gives a capacity that grows exponentially in the dimension rather than linearly.

Where this leaves the design#

R014 makes the same point from another direction: transformers, state space models and their relatives are one construction fixed by three choices. A design that only rearranges those choices has not left the family. Leaving it means denying something the family assumes, and the assumption worth attacking is that memory formation is a regression at all.
